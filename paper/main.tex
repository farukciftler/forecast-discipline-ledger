\begin{abstract}
Agent evaluation is normally episodic: a task is attempted once and scored
pass or fail. We report on a different setting. For 38 consecutive days a
language-model agent maintained a live measurement system with real
consequences under human supervision --- a personal forecasting ledger that
writes dated, sealed, pre-registered predictions and scores them against
realised outcomes. Alongside the forecasts we kept a structured log of every
operational error the agent made while running the process, recording the
error class, the mechanism that caught it, \emph{who} caught it, and how many
days it stayed hidden. We release both datasets.

The forecasting results are negative and were pre-registered. The agent beats
each of three baselines individually but beats all three simultaneously in only
$118/352 = 33.5\%$ of scored rows ($p = 1.0$, one-sided); prediction intervals
are too wide ($86.9\%$ coverage against an $80\%$ target, exact binomial
$p = 2.4\times10^{-4}$); and the up-move probability scores slightly worse than
the sample base rate (Brier skill score $-0.016$).

The error log is the more unusual object. Across 97 records, detection latency
is heavily right-skewed --- median 1 day, mean 9.1, maximum 400 --- and
$37/97 = 38.1\%$ of records are repeats of an earlier one. We name and
illustrate a recurring failure mode of agent-maintained systems: the agent
writes a correct rule into its own documentation and then does not follow it,
because \emph{documentation is not a mechanism}. Four instances were identified,
one of which persisted for 400 days. Finally, we state the limitation that
governs all of this: the log is written by the same agent that made the errors,
so errors it never noticed are absent by construction. We propose a
capture--recapture design that would make the unobserved population estimable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most published evaluation of language-model agents is \emph{episodic}. A
benchmark presents a task, the agent attempts it, and the attempt is scored.
This design answers ``can the agent do X?'' It does not answer a question that
matters more once agents are deployed: \emph{what happens to a system an agent
maintains over months, and how do its failures actually surface?}

Answering that requires a task with three properties that benchmarks usually
lack. It must be continuing rather than one-shot, so that errors have time to
compound or to be caught. It must have consequences, so that the agent has
something to be wrong about. And it must have a human in the loop who
occasionally notices things the agent did not, so that self-report can be
partially checked.

This paper reports on such a task and releases the resulting data. The agent
maintains a forecasting ledger over six financial assets. Each business day it
records point forecasts, $80\%$ intervals and up-move probabilities at 1-day,
1-week and 1-month horizons, before outcomes are known; the ledger seals them
and scores them automatically. The analysis plan was pre-registered and
protocol changes are versioned, so that observations produced under different
rules are not pooled.

Our contribution is not a forecasting method. On the contrary, the forecasting
results are null, and we report them as such. The contribution is the
\emph{operational telemetry} collected alongside: a structured record of every
mistake the agent made while running the process, with detection latency and
attribution.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A released, anonymised, longitudinal dataset of language-model agent
        operational errors with detection mechanism, detecting party, and
        detection latency in days (\S\ref{sec:errors}).
  \item Pre-registered null results on short-horizon forecasting, including
        interval mis-calibration in a specific and correctable direction
        (\S\ref{sec:forecast}).
  \item A named, illustrated failure mode --- \emph{rules written but not
        mechanised} --- with four instances and a measured repeat rate
        (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item A design proposal that would make the unobserved error population
        estimable rather than merely acknowledged (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{The task.} A daily process over seven assets (six forecastable,
pseudonymised \texttt{A1}--\texttt{A7}): collect prices from machine-readable
sources, record a market-state snapshot, resolve any forecasts that have come
due, scan the day's news, write new forecasts, and commit. Steps that must be
deterministic are performed by a fixed engine; steps that require judgement
are performed by the agent. The engine never fetches data and never produces
forecasts; the agent never computes scores. This split is deliberate: the
arithmetic must not hallucinate, and neither must the data collection.

\paragraph{What is recorded.} Every forecast carries a point estimate, an
$80\%$ interval, an up-move probability, a stated confidence, the evidence
items it was derived from, and an invalidating condition. Resolution is
automatic against the realised value and against three baselines: \emph{naive}
(no change), \emph{drift} (mean of the last five observed changes) and
\emph{momentum} (the last observed change). All three use only information
available at forecast time.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} The analysis plan fixes, in advance, which
hypothesis is primary, which test is applied, the minimum $n$, and the
reporting order. Findings that are not in the plan are labelled exploratory
and are not counted as evidence. Rule changes increment a protocol version and
split the pool.

\paragraph{Scale.} 38 forecast days (2026-08-01 to 2026-09-07), 659 forecasts
written, 383 scored, 97 error records. This is an interim report; the ledger
continues.

\section{Forecast results are null}\label{sec:forecast}

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Baseline & beaten & rate & $p$ (one-sided) \\
\midrule
naive             & 244/383 & 0.637 & $4.5\times10^{-8}$ \\
drift             & 225/352 & 0.639 & $9.8\times10^{-8}$ \\
momentum          & 265/352 & 0.753 & $2.4\times10^{-22}$ \\
\textbf{all three simultaneously} & \textbf{118/352} & \textbf{0.335} & \textbf{1.0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Beating any single baseline is easy in a trending sample. Beating all
three at once is the number that matters, and it fails. Wilson 95\% CI for the
joint row: $(0.288,\,0.386)$.}
\label{tab:baselines}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} states the central negative result. Reporting only
the naive comparison would have supported a headline of ``the agent beats the
baseline, $p < 10^{-7}$''. That headline would have been an artefact of a
one-directional sample: $70\%$ of realised moves in the window were upward, so
any small positive point forecast beats a zero-change baseline mechanically.
The joint criterion was fixed in advance for exactly this reason.

\paragraph{Intervals are mis-calibrated in a correctable direction.}
Coverage is $333/383 = 86.9\%$ (Wilson 95\% CI $0.832$--$0.900$) against an
$80\%$ target; the exact binomial probability of coverage this high or higher
under a true $0.80$ is $2.4\times10^{-4}$. The intervals are too wide. Unlike
the point forecasts, this is a measurable and fixable defect, and it is where
the ledger's own ongoing work is directed.

\paragraph{Probabilities carry no skill.} Mean Brier score is $0.2133$
($n=360$) against $0.2100$ for a constant forecast at the sample base rate of
$0.70$, giving a Brier skill score of $-0.016$. The stated probabilities are,
if anything, slightly worse than knowing how often the market went up.

\paragraph{Power.} These figures overstate the evidence. The assets move in
correlated blocks --- two equity funds share roughly $70\%$ of their exposure,
two precious-metal instruments track the same underlying --- so the effective
number of independent observations per day is roughly three, not the nominal
count. With 38 days, no forecasting conclusion here should be read as more
than ``not demonstrated''.

\section{Operational failure telemetry}\label{sec:errors}

Every operational mistake is logged with a controlled vocabulary: error class
(8 values), the mechanism that caught it (10 values), the detecting party
(agent or human), the direction of the resulting correction, and the number of
days between the event and its detection.

\end{tabular}
\caption{Error log summary. Latency is heavily right-skewed: most errors are

\label{tab:errors}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Two features stand out.} First, latency is not merely skewed but
bimodal in character: the median is one day while the mean is nine and the
maximum is 400. Routine cross-checking catches errors of value almost
immediately; a different kind of error survives it entirely. Second, more than
a third of records ($38.1\%$) are repeats of an earlier record --- the agent
returns to failure classes it has already documented.

\paragraph{Self-correction appears directionally unbiased.} Of the records
whose effect on the ledger's own scores was classified, 9 were favourable to
the agent and 10 unfavourable (two-sided exact $p = 1.0$; favourable share
$0.474$, CI95 $0.273$--$0.683$). This is the one result here where a
\emph{significant} finding would have been bad news: a systematic skew toward
self-favouring corrections would indicate that corrections are chosen after
seeing which way they cut. No such skew is detected, though the test is
underpowered.

\section{Rules are not mechanisms}\label{sec:mechanism}

The recurring pattern behind the long-latency tail is specific enough to name.
Repeatedly, the agent diagnosed a real problem correctly, wrote the correct
rule into its own documentation, and then failed to apply it --- because a
document is not an enforcement point. Four instances were identified in the
released log:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llr p{5.6cm}}
\toprule
Record & Class & Latency & The rule that existed but was not enforced \\
\midrule
K93 & process     & 400 d & ``Index values must be cross-checked against the
previous close.'' Written after a $5\%$ index mis-read; no code ever checked. \\
K91 & process     & 18 d  & Three rules --- shadow intervals, an adversarial
pre-commit review, and a second ledger's snapshot --- all documented, none in
the daily runner. \\
K80 & measurement & 0 d   & Market-state fields silently disappeared between
days; only 7 of 80+ fields were validated. \\
K87 & measurement & 1 d   & A prior diagnosis of a data-provider anomaly was
itself wrong; the behavioural rule survived, its stated cause did not. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Instances of the pattern. In each case the knowledge existed and was
written down; what was missing was an automatic check.}
\label{tab:mechanism}
\end{table}

The cost is measurable. In one case a documented interval-recording rule was
followed on the day it was written, ignored for the next three days, and
resumed only when someone remembered it; those three days are permanently
absent from the hypothesis pool they were meant to fill, because the quantity
cannot be reconstructed after the fact.

\paragraph{Why this should generalise.} The pattern does not depend on the
particular agent or task. It depends on a structural asymmetry: an agent that
writes its own operating documentation is also the reader of that
documentation, and reading is not enforced. Every rule that survives only as
prose is a rule contingent on the agent remembering it at the moment it
applies --- which is precisely the moment the agent is distracted by the task
that caused the rule to be written.

\paragraph{A failed exploratory test.} We expected errors of \emph{absence}
(a missing field, an unenforced rule) to hide longer than errors of
\emph{wrong value}, since a wrong number can be cross-checked and a missing
one cannot. Classifying by error class, absence-like records
($n = 36$) have median latency $1.0$ days and mean $18.6$; the remainder
($n = 61$) have median $1.0$ and mean $3.4$. A Mann--Whitney test gives
$z = -1.60$, two-sided $p = 0.11$: \textbf{not supported}. The difference lives
entirely in the tail, driven by a single 400-day observation. We report this
because it was run, and because the hypothesis was formed \emph{after} looking
at the data; it is registered for prospective testing rather than claimed.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The log is self-reported.} It is written by the same agent that
made the errors. Errors the agent never noticed are absent by construction,
and no amount of internal analysis can recover them. The 20 human-detected
records give a hint of the miss rate but not an estimate: we do not record
whether the agent \emph{would} have caught what the human caught, so the two
detectors cannot be compared.

\paragraph{A design that would fix this.} Capture--recapture, standard in
epidemiology and software defect estimation, requires only that overlap be
recorded: for each error, whether each detector found it independently. With
overlap, the unobserved population becomes estimable rather than merely
acknowledged. We have added the field prospectively; it carries no data yet.

\paragraph{Single agent, single task, small $n$.} One agent, one task, one
supervisor, 38 days. Nothing here establishes a rate that transfers to other
agents. The dataset's value is that it exists and is open, not that it is
representative.

\paragraph{Classification is partly retrospective.} The controlled vocabulary
was introduced after 75 records had accumulated; those records were mapped to
it \emph{after} the data was visible. The released log therefore distinguishes
an exploratory pool from a confirmatory pool beginning at the record where the
vocabulary was fixed. Tests intended as evidence use the confirmatory pool
only, which currently holds 20 records.

The repository contains the pre-registration, methods, codebook, case
descriptions, and a dependency-free script that regenerates every number in
this paper from the released CSVs. Every figure and table above is produced by
\texttt{paper/compute\_numbers.py}.

The exporter that produces the anonymised release and the independent auditor
that verifies it are deliberately \emph{not} published: the exporter carries
the alias mapping and the auditor carries the forbidden-term list, so
publishing either would defeat the anonymisation.

\paragraph{What is withheld.} Amounts, balances, unit counts, price levels,
institution, instrument, vendor and person names, and free text. Assets appear
as \texttt{A1}--\texttt{A7}, the forecasting models as \modelA{} and \modelB{},
and country-specific market variables under role labels; global public
benchmarks keep their usual names. The accepted residual risk is that a fund's
return series could in principle be matched against public NAV series and the
instrument identified, which reveals nothing about any person.

\section{Conclusion}

An agent ran a real measurement system for 38 days and produced forecasts with
no demonstrated skill. That is the uninteresting half of the result, and it was
pre-registered so that it could not be quietly reframed.

The interesting half is what the process recorded about itself. Errors are
caught quickly on average and occasionally not at all for over a year; more
than a third recur; and the corrections the agent applies to its own records
show no detectable self-serving bias. The dominant long-latency failure is not
a wrong answer but an unenforced rule --- knowledge that the agent possessed,
documented, and did not act on.

If agents are going to maintain systems, the useful question is not only how
often they are right. It is how their being wrong becomes visible, how long
that takes, and whether writing the lesson down is enough. On the evidence
here, it is not.
